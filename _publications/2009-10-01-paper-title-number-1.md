Gold nanomaterials have also been explored as carriers for therapeutic agents and as potential components of combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy approaches. Nevertheless, the available evidence is still dominated by preclinical investigations, and the reported outcomes differ depending on nanoparticle dimensions, surface modification, experimental tumor models, radiation conditions, and delivery methods. Several issues—including organ distribution, long-term biological effects, reproducible manufacturing, and regulatory requirements—continue to hinder clinical translation. Conclusion: Gold nanoparticles offer promising possibilities for cancer diagnosis and treatment, although their clinical utility requires further validation. Future investigations should prioritize standardized material characterization, reproducible experimental protocols, comprehensive safety assessment, and stronger evidence of clinical benefit.

1. Introduction

Nanotechnology focuses on the design, manipulation, and application of materials at extremely small dimensions, commonly within the nanoscale range. Its development has created opportunities in medicine by enabling the engineering of materials with properties suitable for biological detection, targeted delivery, and therapeutic intervention. In oncology, nanotechnology is being investigated as a means of improving the localization of therapeutic agents and addressing limitations associated with conventional cancer treatment. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have attracted particular attention because their physicochemical characteristics can be modified through changes in particle morphology, dimensions, and surface chemistry. These features make them candidates for investigation in cancer imaging, drug delivery, photothermal applications, and radiation-based treatment strategies. Their interactions with electromagnetic radiation and their potential to carry functional molecules have further expanded their relevance to experimental cancer research. However, the performance of gold nanomaterials depends on their formulation and the biological environment in which they are used. Consequently, their potential advantages must be evaluated through systematic studies of imaging performance, therapeutic effectiveness, biodistribution, and safety.

2. Methods and Research Selection

This article adopts a narrative-review approach to examine research on gold nanomaterials in cancer imaging and treatment. A reproducible literature-search strategy should identify relevant publications through recognized scientific databases, such as PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, using an explicitly stated search date and publication-period limit. The search strategy may combine terms including “gold nanoparticles,” “cancer imaging,” “computed tomography,” “magnetic resonance imaging,” “radiotherapy,” “radiosensitization,” “chemotherapy,” and “chemoradiotherapy.” Search combinations should be adapted to the indexing and search functions of each database. The literature should then be screened in stages, beginning with titles and abstracts and proceeding to full-text assessment for potentially eligible publications. Studies may be considered relevant when they investigate gold nanoparticle applications in cancer detection, imaging contrast, radiation response, therapeutic delivery, or combined treatment. Particular attention should be given to the distinction between in-vitro experiments, animal studies, and human clinical investigations. Publications should be assessed for methodological relevance, the suitability of their experimental models, and the strength of the evidence supporting their conclusions. The final selection process, including the databases searched, search dates, eligibility criteria, and number of studies included, must reflect the review that was actually conducted. These details should not be reported as completed procedures unless they have been independently verified. The review considered both original research articles and previously published reviews to examine the underlying mechanisms, experimental observations, and early progress toward clinical application. Studies were excluded when they were not peer-reviewed, were published in languages outside the review's eligibility criteria, did not address cancer-related applications, or lacked a specific focus on gold nanoparticles. As a narrative review, the objective was to provide a qualitative synthesis of representative studies rather than conduct a formal systematic review or quantitative meta-analysis. The available evidence was therefore organized into major themes, including diagnostic imaging, radiation enhancement, chemotherapy-related applications, and barriers to clinical implementation. Where sufficient information was available, findings from cell-based experiments, animal models, and human studies were considered separately to avoid treating evidence from different research stages as equivalent.

3. Discussion

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) are nanoscale materials whose dimensions, morphology, and surrounding chemical environment influence their physical and optical behavior. Dispersions of these particles can exhibit different colors because their interaction with light depends on factors such as particle size, shape, aggregation, and surface properties. These characteristics have encouraged their investigation in cancer diagnosis and treatment. Several features are particularly relevant to biomedical applications, including the ability to modify the particle surface, tailor optical responses, and influence interactions with ionizing radiation (Figure 1). However, the biological safety of a gold nanoparticle formulation cannot be inferred solely from the generally recognized chemical stability of bulk gold. Safety depends on particle dimensions, geometry, coating composition, administered dose, route of exposure, tissue distribution, and the duration of retention within the body. Another important characteristic is the localized surface plasmon resonance associated with many gold nanostructures. Their optical response can be adjusted by changing particle geometry and the surrounding dielectric environment. Surface modification using suitable chemical groups can also facilitate the attachment of targeting molecules or therapeutic agents. Such modifications may improve colloidal stability or influence biological interactions, although their effectiveness must be established experimentally. Gold nanoparticles have also attracted attention as potential radiation-enhancing materials. Their high atomic number affects their interaction with ionizing radiation, providing a physical basis for investigating whether they can increase radiation-induced effects in tumors. Nevertheless, the magnitude of any enhancement depends on radiation energy, nanoparticle concentration and localization, cellular uptake, and the biological mechanisms responsible for treatment response. Their performance must therefore be assessed against appropriate controls and established treatment methods. Gold nanoparticles contain a high atomic number element, a characteristic that influences their interaction with ionizing radiation. Under appropriate irradiation conditions, these interactions may increase local energy deposition and contribute to enhanced radiation-induced effects in tumor cells. Their nanoscale dimensions may also facilitate transport through some tumor-associated vascular structures, although actual tumor accumulation depends on vascular permeability, particle properties, and biological barriers. These characteristics have encouraged continued investigation of gold nanoparticles as potential agents for cancer detection and treatment.

3.1 Nanotechnology for Cancer Imaging

Preclinical research has explored gold nanoparticles as contrast-enhancing materials for several imaging techniques, with the aim of improving tumor detection and supporting treatment planning [1]. Their distinctive physicochemical properties have made them potential alternatives or complements to conventional contrast agents used in diagnostic imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT). Conventional iodine-containing agents are widely used in clinical CT examinations, but their performance is influenced by factors such as circulation time, contrast concentration, acquisition parameters, and patient-specific considerations [3–5]. These limitations have encouraged researchers to investigate alternative materials that may provide useful X-ray attenuation characteristics under suitable conditions. Gold has a higher atomic number than iodine, which affects its interaction with X-rays and provides a physical basis for studying gold-based contrast enhancement [8]. Its potential imaging performance, however, depends not only on atomic number but also on the concentration of gold within the target tissue, the incident X-ray spectrum, nanoparticle distribution, and the surrounding biological environment. In experimental settings, gold nanoparticles have therefore been investigated for their ability to improve the visibility of selected tissues and potentially support image-guided cancer treatment. The extent of any advantage over established contrast agents must be determined through controlled comparisons that account for imaging dose, signal-to-noise ratio, pharmacokinetics, toxicity, and practical clinical requirements. The translation of gold-based contrast agents into routine imaging also requires evidence that sufficient quantities can reach the intended tissue while maintaining acceptable background contrast and biological safety. Accordingly, improved X-ray attenuation should be regarded as a promising material property rather than, by itself, proof of superior diagnostic accuracy or clinical benefit.

Gold has attracted attention as a potential X-ray contrast material because its mass attenuation characteristics differ from those of conventional contrast elements such as iodine. The magnitude of this difference depends on photon energy and the physical conditions under which attenuation is measured. Gold-based formulations have therefore been investigated for CT imaging, particularly in energy ranges where their interaction with X-rays may be advantageous. The potential benefits of gold nanoparticles extend beyond their intrinsic attenuation properties. Their surfaces can be modified with suitable biomolecules to influence stability, biological interactions, and tissue distribution. Following intravenous administration, nanoparticle accumulation within tumors may occur through passive transport associated with abnormal tumor vasculature or through appropriately designed targeting strategies. The degree of accumulation varies with particle characteristics, tumor biology, and the delivery system. Preclinical investigations have reported improvements in CT contrast under selected experimental conditions; however, the clinical significance of these findings requires further validation.

![Gold and Iodine Attenuation Curves.png](https://cdn.steemitimages.com/DQmeu94PnbNJgxSJBTEahATMwT3cpKQRraQ7twSUd1TECnh/Gold%20and%20Iodine%20Attenuation%20Curves.png)

Gold nanoparticles also exhibit optical responses associated with localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR). This phenomenon arises from the collective oscillation of conduction electrons in response to incident electromagnetic radiation. When the excitation frequency is appropriate for a particular nanostructure, its optical absorption and scattering can increase substantially. The resonance characteristics depend on nanoparticle geometry, dimensions, composition, aggregation, and the dielectric properties of the surrounding medium. By adjusting these parameters, researchers can modify the wavelength range in which a gold nanostructure interacts strongly with light. This tunability is relevant to optical sensing, photoacoustic imaging, and photothermal therapy. During photothermal conversion, absorbed optical energy is transferred into heat through non-radiative relaxation processes. The resulting temperature distribution depends on the illumination conditions, nanoparticle concentration, heat transfer within the surrounding medium, and the duration of exposure. Consequently, localized optical absorption can support investigations of spatially controlled heating, but the actual therapeutic effect must be confirmed through temperature measurements and appropriate biological experiments. As a result, appropriately designed nanoparticles may offer opportunities for localized imaging and the investigation of tumor-specific diagnostic strategies. Gold-based contrast agents have been explored as potential molecular imaging platforms for tumors that are difficult to characterize using conventional anatomical imaging alone. By attaching tumor-recognition molecules, such as selected antibodies, to nanoparticle surfaces, researchers aim to increase the preferential association of gold with cancer cells expressing the corresponding targets. Such localization may produce differences in X-ray attenuation between targeted and non-targeted tissues. However, the magnitude of contrast enhancement depends on target expression, nanoparticle delivery, tissue distribution, and the imaging protocol. Preclinical investigations have also examined antibody-functionalized gold nanoparticles for identifying tumors that are difficult to distinguish using standard CT. For example, epidermal growth factor-related targeting strategies have been explored in experimental tumor models. Other studies have investigated immune-cell-associated targets, including CD4, to examine whether nanoparticle-based contrast could improve the visualization of lymphatic structures and assist treatment planning. These approaches remain dependent on the specificity of the targeting molecule and the biological characteristics of the tissue under investigation. Beyond CT, gold nanomaterials have been incorporated into experimental platforms designed to combine multiple imaging modalities. Conventional gold nanoparticles do not inherently provide the strong magnetic relaxation effects required of standard MRI contrast agents. Consequently, MRI applications generally require additional magnetic components or hybrid structures that combine gold with suitable magnetic materials. Gold–iron oxide formulations, for instance, have been investigated to explore complementary CT and MRI information. Other engineered hybrid nanoparticles have also been studied for molecular imaging and biological detection. Taken together, these investigations indicate that gold-based nanostructures may contribute to contrast enhancement under selected experimental conditions. Nevertheless, much of the available evidence comes from laboratory experiments and animal models. More extensive clinical research is needed to establish diagnostic accuracy, safety, reproducibility, and any advantage over existing imaging methods.

3.2. Nanotechnology for Radiation Sensitization

Radiation oncology employs ionizing radiation to damage malignant cells while attempting to preserve surrounding healthy tissues. Modern treatment strategies use different radiation sources, dose distributions, fractionation schedules, and delivery technologies to improve tumor control and limit adverse effects. One persistent challenge is the unintended exposure of nearby normal tissues to radiation. Depending on the treatment site, dose, and individual patient characteristics, this exposure can contribute to tissue injury, impaired organ function, and other complications. Improving the therapeutic balance therefore requires approaches that increase tumor response without producing an unacceptable increase in damage to healthy structures. Nanotechnology has emerged as a research direction for addressing this challenge. Investigators are examining whether engineered nanomaterials can modify radiation interactions, influence cellular responses to irradiation, or deliver sensitizing agents preferentially to tumor tissue. The objective is to improve the effectiveness of radiation treatment while limiting unnecessary exposure and toxicity in surrounding organs. However, the magnitude and selectivity of these effects must be established experimentally, and findings from preclinical models should not be interpreted as proof of clinical benefit. Experimental research has identified gold nanoparticles as potential candidates for improving the response of cancer cells to radiation-based treatment. Radiation sensitization refers to an increase in the susceptibility of tumor cells to radiation-induced damage. Gold nanoparticles have been investigated for this purpose because of their interactions with high-energy photons and their potential to modify energy deposition within irradiated tissue. Depending on the radiation spectrum and nanoparticle distribution, these interactions may contribute to localized physical and biological effects. The principal objective of radiosensitization is to improve tumor control while avoiding unnecessary injury to surrounding healthy tissues. Gold nanoparticles may also contribute to image-guided treatment by increasing X-ray contrast in regions where sufficient material accumulates. This could help researchers investigate tumor localization and improve the delineation of treatment targets. However, imaging enhancement and therapeutic radiosensitization are distinct outcomes and should be evaluated separately. Gold has a high atomic number, which influences its interaction with ionizing radiation. Under suitable conditions, gold-containing materials can alter local radiation interactions and energy deposition. The extent of this effect depends on photon energy, particle concentration, intracellular localization, and the surrounding biological medium. Consequently, the presence of gold does not guarantee a uniform increase in radiation damage across all tumor cells. Preclinical studies have investigated whether gold nanoparticle administration can improve outcomes following irradiation. Some animal experiments have reported differences in tumor response between nanoparticle-treated and control groups. Such findings require careful interpretation because outcomes may depend on the tumor model, nanoparticle formulation, treatment schedule, radiation dose, and the concentration achieved within the tumor relative to healthy organs. One important parameter is the tumor-to-normal-tissue concentration ratio. Preferential accumulation within a tumor could potentially improve the balance between tumor irradiation and normal-tissue exposure. Nevertheless, this distribution is not universal and can vary considerably with particle size, surface chemistry, vascular characteristics, and biological clearance. The therapeutic value of gold-based radiosensitizers therefore requires systematic evaluation of biodistribution, dose enhancement, toxicity, and long-term outcomes.

Although experimental findings have indicated that gold nanoparticles may improve radiation-based tumor control, the underlying mechanisms and the consistency of these effects remain incompletely understood. Differences in nanoparticle geometry, particle dimensions, concentration, cellular characteristics, and irradiation conditions may contribute to the variation observed across studies. Experiments involving HeLa cells have also explored potential radiosensitizing effects, but the available findings do not yet establish a consistent response across different experimental settings. Further evidence is therefore required before the approach can be reliably evaluated in clinical trials. Several practical challenges must be addressed before gold nanoparticle-based radiosensitization can be incorporated into routine treatment. These include delivering the particles to the intended tumor region, obtaining sufficiently uniform intratumoral distribution, and maintaining an appropriate concentration for the required treatment period. Nanoparticle persistence in circulation and the efficiency of transport into deeper tumor regions may also influence therapeutic performance. In addition, the relationship between radiation dose and nanoparticle concentration has not been standardized across experimental models. Carefully controlled studies are consequently needed to establish reproducible treatment conditions and determine whether the potential benefits justify clinical investigation. Researchers have examined gold nanoparticle radiosensitization under both kilovoltage and megavoltage irradiation. Kilovoltage radiation is commonly associated with lower-energy X-rays, whereas megavoltage beams are widely used in external-beam radiotherapy. Because the interaction of radiation with gold depends on photon energy and the surrounding medium, the magnitude and mechanisms of radiosensitization may differ between these regimes. Experimental studies have reported enhanced radiation responses with gold nanoparticles under selected irradiation conditions, including megavoltage energies relevant to modern radiotherapy. Investigations in breast cancer models have compared radiation responses at different energy ranges and explored the possibility of improving tumor control through nanoparticle administration. Other studies have reported enhanced tumor-cell killing and have examined whether similar effects occur under kilovoltage and megavoltage irradiation. These observations support further investigation, although the relative contribution of physical dose enhancement and biological mechanisms remains an important research question. Beyond their physical interactions with radiation, gold nanoparticles may influence cellular processes associated with radiation-induced damage. Proposed mechanisms include alterations in cell-cycle progression, impaired cellular recovery, increased apoptosis, and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that can contribute to DNA damage. The importance of each pathway may vary with cell type, nanoparticle formulation, intracellular localization, and irradiation conditions. Cell-based experiments have suggested that several biological pathways may contribute to the observed radiosensitizing response. Nevertheless, the extent to which these mechanisms produce reliable therapeutic benefits in living organisms remains uncertain. Differences in tumor biology, nanoparticle accumulation, radiation delivery, and normal-tissue responses can lead to substantial variation between experimental models. Animal investigations have examined whether the administration of gold nanoparticles can influence radiation-induced tumor responses. For example, studies using mammary tumor models have investigated nanoparticle accumulation and subsequent radiation-associated tumor-cell damage. Such findings provide a basis for further research, but conclusions about therapeutic efficacy must account for the experimental design, administered dose, treatment schedule, toxicity, and duration of follow-up. Although encouraging experimental results have been reported, the mechanisms responsible for the observed tumor response are not yet fully established. Studies suggest that the effects of gold nanoparticles may vary with particle size, shape, concentration, cellular characteristics, and irradiation conditions. These uncertainties highlight the need for standardized experimental protocols and further investigation before gold-based radiosensitizers can be evaluated reliably in clinical trials. From a physical perspective, gold nanoparticles may influence radiation interactions through several mechanisms, including photon scattering, photoelectric absorption, and the production of secondary electrons. The relative contribution of each process depends on the incident photon energy and the material environment. These interactions can alter local energy deposition and may contribute to radiation-induced damage in the immediate surroundings of the nanoparticles.

Experimental studies have further examined the biological consequences of gold nanoparticle accumulation in tumor cells. Research using melanoma models has investigated whether nanoparticle administration can increase radiation-associated cell death, delay tumor growth, and influence survival outcomes in tumor-bearing animals. However, such findings must be interpreted in the context of the specific experimental design, treatment protocol, and nanoparticle formulation. Additional preclinical investigations have explored gold-based radiosensitization in different cancer models, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, prostate cancer, ovarian cancer, glioma, and brain tumors. These studies indicate that the potential application of gold nanoparticles is not restricted to a single tumor type. Nevertheless, differences in tumor biology, particle delivery, cellular uptake, and radiation sensitivity may lead to different outcomes across cancer models. Further work is needed to determine which formulations and treatment conditions provide reproducible benefits, how tumor accumulation can be improved, and whether the resulting therapeutic gains outweigh potential risks to healthy tissues. Comparisons with standard radiotherapy, together with systematic assessment of toxicity and long-term outcomes, will be important for establishing the clinical relevance of gold nanoparticle-based radiosensitization. Experimental investigations have reported increased DNA damage, tumor-cell destruction, and potential improvements in treatment outcomes following the use of gold nanoparticles with radiation. These findings indicate that gold-based formulations may improve tumor response under selected conditions. However, the magnitude of radiosensitization differs between studies and depends on nanoparticle characteristics, intracellular distribution, radiation energy, and the tumor model used. Consequently, the results cannot be directly generalized to clinical treatment, and human evidence remains limited. Proton radiotherapy has also been investigated in combination with gold nanoparticles. In preclinical prostate cancer models, the presence of gold nanoparticles has been associated with changes in radiation-induced ionization and cellular survival following proton exposure. Some studies have reported improved treatment responses compared with proton irradiation alone. Proposed explanations include interactions between incident protons and gold, the generation of secondary particles and electrons, and subsequent energy deposition within biological tissue. The magnitude of these effects depends on experimental conditions and requires independent validation. Other investigations have examined the relationship between gold nanoparticle administration, proton irradiation, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Experimental findings have suggested increased oxidative stress and changes in tumor response in cell-based and animal models. Nevertheless, further research is required to distinguish physical dose enhancement from biological effects and to determine whether these mechanisms produce a reproducible therapeutic advantage.

3.3. Nanotechnology in Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is an established treatment option for many cancers and is frequently combined with radiotherapy when clinically appropriate. Such combined treatment may improve disease control in selected settings, but it can also increase adverse effects compared with individual treatment modalities. This has encouraged research into nanotechnology-based approaches that could improve drug delivery, modify treatment response, or reduce exposure of healthy tissues. Gold nanoparticles are being investigated as components of drug-delivery systems and as potential partners in combined chemotherapy and radiation treatment. Their surfaces can be modified to carry selected therapeutic molecules, while their physicochemical properties may influence drug loading, cellular uptake, and biological distribution. Preclinical studies have explored whether these characteristics can improve treatment interactions or alter toxicity profiles. However, any reduction in toxicity or improvement in efficacy must be established for each formulation and therapeutic agent. The distribution of nanoparticle-based drug systems depends on particle dimensions, surface chemistry, circulation behavior, and the properties of the tumor microenvironment. Some formulations may accumulate in tumors through transport associated with abnormal tumor vasculature, whereas others rely on specific targeting strategies. Such accumulation is variable and should not be interpreted as selective delivery to every tumor. Nanoparticle size and surface modification can also influence clearance from the body. Depending on their physicochemical characteristics, gold-containing formulations may interact with the mononuclear phagocyte system and accumulate in organs involved in biological clearance. These processes affect circulation time, tissue retention, and the potential for long-term exposure. Therefore, the pharmacokinetics of a nanoparticle-bound drug may differ substantially from those of the corresponding free drug. Although these delivery systems offer opportunities for modifying drug distribution, their therapeutic value depends on achieving an appropriate balance between tumor accumulation, drug release, treatment effectiveness, and biological safety. Comprehensive evaluation of biodistribution, clearance, toxicity, and treatment outcomes is essential before any formulation can be considered for clinical use. Although nanoparticle-based drug delivery may reduce exposure of healthy tissues in certain experimental settings, its benefits depend strongly on the formulation, biological distribution, and extent of tumor accumulation. Evidence from human studies remains limited, and the clinical significance of these potential advantages has not yet been established. Gold nanoparticles have been investigated for combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy partly because of the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Tumor blood vessels often develop abnormally during angiogenesis, producing irregular vascular structures with increased permeability. Depending on particle characteristics, these vascular abnormalities may permit some nanoparticles to leave the bloodstream and enter tumor tissue (Figure 6). In many tumors, lymphatic drainage is also impaired, which can contribute to the retention of materials within the tumor microenvironment. The combination of altered vascular permeability and reduced clearance has been proposed as one mechanism for nanoparticle accumulation. However, the magnitude of the EPR effect varies considerably between tumor types, experimental models, and individual patients. Radiotherapy and other treatment-related changes may further influence tumor vascular properties and the distribution of therapeutic agents. In selected preclinical investigations, nanoparticle accumulation has been associated with improved delivery of therapeutic compounds and changes in treatment response. Gold-based delivery systems may therefore offer opportunities to investigate whether effective treatment can be achieved with reduced drug exposure. Nevertheless, dose reduction and decreased toxicity must be demonstrated experimentally rather than assumed from nanoparticle accumulation alone.

3.4. Current Limitations

Despite growing interest in gold nanoparticles for cancer diagnosis and treatment, several scientific and practical challenges remain. Their performance depends on material properties, biological interactions, tumor characteristics, and the method of administration. Findings from laboratory experiments do not necessarily predict outcomes in patients, particularly when tumor vascular structure, nanoparticle distribution, and clearance differ substantially between experimental models and human disease.

Clinical translation of gold nanoparticle-based cancer treatments remains at an early stage. Most available findings originate from preclinical experiments, while direct comparisons between studies are complicated by variations in particle morphology, size, surface coating, tumor models, administration routes, and irradiation conditions. Therefore, results obtained with one formulation should not automatically be generalized to other gold nanoparticle systems. Safety is another major consideration. Although bulk gold has been used in selected medical applications, its nanoscale formulations may behave differently in biological environments. Their effects depend on particle dimensions, surface chemistry, administered dose, route of administration, circulation time, organ distribution, and elimination. Potential concerns include prolonged retention in the reticuloendothelial system, delayed clearance by the liver, and unintended accumulation in other tissues. These factors require systematic assessment for each formulation. Efficient tumor delivery remains a further challenge. Nanoparticle accumulation is frequently discussed in relation to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. However, vascular permeability and lymphatic function vary substantially between tumor types and individual patients. Consequently, EPR-mediated accumulation cannot be assumed to occur consistently in human tumors, limiting the predictability of intratumoral delivery and therapeutic response. The available clinical evidence is also limited. Only a relatively small number of nanoparticle formulations have progressed to early-stage clinical investigation, and their results must be interpreted in the context of their composition and intended application. Gold-containing systems, including CYT-6091 and AuroShell particles, illustrate efforts to translate gold-based nanotechnology into clinical research. In contrast, NBTXR3 is based on hafnium oxide rather than gold and should be treated as a separate nanomaterial comparator. For wider clinical implementation, gold nanoparticle manufacturing will need to achieve greater reproducibility and tighter control of particle dimensions, surface functionalization, purity, stability, and batch-to-batch variation. Reliable characterization, standardized quality-control procedures, and well-designed clinical studies will be essential to establish whether these systems provide a meaningful advantage over existing diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.
